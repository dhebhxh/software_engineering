# Evaluation 
To evaluate our game, we conducted a user study comparing two levels (Level 1 and Level 2), aiming to assess usability, workload, and overall gameplay experience. Both qualitative and quantitative methods were used, including the System Usability Scale (SUS), NASA Task Load Index (NASA-TLX), and statistical testing.

## 1. Qualitative evaluation 
From a qualitative perspective, most users found Level 1 intuitive and easy to understand, making it suitable as an introductory level. Players reported that they could quickly grasp the basic controls and mechanics, which provided a smooth learning experience. In contrast, Level 2 was perceived as more challenging and required more strategic thinking, particularly in using the recording system effectively. Several participants noted that the recording mechanic was innovative and engaging, but some found it initially difficult to fully understand. This suggests that while the mechanic adds depth to gameplay, clearer guidance or tutorial elements could improve usability. Additionally, some users reported confusion when interacting with clones, indicating potential issues with feedback clarity and system consistency.

## 2. Quantitative evaluation
For the quantitative evaluation, SUS scores were analysed for both levels. The average SUS score for Level 1 was approximately 71, while Level 2 had an average of approximately 72. These values indicate generally good usability across both levels. However, a Wilcoxon signed-rank test (N = 10, α = 0.05) showed no significant difference between the two levels (W = 21). This suggests that despite differences in perceived difficulty, the overall usability of the system remains consistent. In other words, increasing difficulty did not negatively impact usability, which indicates a robust design.

NASA-TLX results were used to assess perceived workload. The average workload score for Level 1 was approximately 39, while Level 2 had a slightly higher average of approximately 47. Although Level 2 appears more demanding, statistical analysis using the Wilcoxon test (W = 10) also indicated no significant difference between the two levels. This suggests that while players experienced increased challenge, the workload remained within an acceptable range. Mental demand and effort tended to be higher in Level 2, while frustration levels varied between users but did not show a consistent increase.

Overall, the results demonstrate that the game successfully differentiates between difficulty levels without significantly affecting usability or workload. Level 1 provides a clear and accessible introduction, while Level 2 increases complexity and engagement. However, the lack of significant differences in both SUS and NASA-TLX results suggests that the difficulty progression may not be strong enough to create a clearly distinct experience for all players. Adjustments to difficulty scaling or clearer feedback mechanisms could further enhance player experience.

## 3. Test description (temporary)
The system was tested through a combination of functional testing and user testing. Core mechanics such as player movement, jumping, collision detection, and the recording system were verified through repeated manual testing during development. Edge cases, including incorrect clone behaviour and interaction failures, were also examined to ensure system stability. In addition, user testing sessions were conducted where participants played both levels and completed SUS and NASA-TLX questionnaires. This approach allowed us to evaluate both technical correctness and user experience, providing a comprehensive assessment of the system.
